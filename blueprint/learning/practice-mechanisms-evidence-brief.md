# Practice Mechanisms Evidence Brief

## Executive Summary

This evidence brief synthesizes meta-analyses and systematic reviews on four core learning science practice mechanisms: retrieval practice (testing effect), distributed practice (spacing effect), interleaving, and worked examples. Across all mechanisms, consistent moderate effect sizes (d ≈ 0.5-0.6) are observed, with synergistic effects when combined.

---

## 1. Dunlosky et al. (2013): Learning Technique Utility Rankings

### Exact Citation

**Authors:** John Dunlosky, Katherine A. Rawson, Elizabeth J. Marsh, Mitchell J. Nathan, and Daniel T. Willingham

**Year:** 2013

**Title:** "Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques: Promising Directions From Cognitive and Educational Psychology"

**Journal:** Psychological Science in the Public Interest

**Volume:** 14(1)

**Pages:** 4-58

**DOI:** 10.1177/1529100612453266

### Learning Technique Utility Ratings

#### High Utility (Strong Evidence)

**Practice Testing (Retrieval Practice)**
- Benefits learners of different ages and abilities
- Robust across many criterion tasks and educational contexts
- Effect sizes: Immediate performance 53% vs 36%; Delayed (1 week) 35% vs 4%
- Karpicke & Roediger (2008): 80% vs 36% on final test 1 week later

**Distributed Practice (Spacing Effect)**
- Benefits learners of different ages and abilities
- Robust across materials and long delays
- Cepeda et al. (2006) meta-analysis: Spaced 47% vs Massed 37% recall

#### Moderate Utility (Promising but Limited Evidence)

**Elaborative Interrogation**
- Benefits generalize across some variables
- Limited evaluation in educational contexts

**Self-Explanation**
- Benefits generalize across some variables
- Insufficient research on durability across educationally relevant delays

**Interleaved Practice**
- Dramatic effects on math skills learning and retention
- Literature currently small but promising
- Effect size: 43% boost in accuracy on delayed tests

#### Low Utility (Weak or Limited Evidence)

- Summarization, Highlighting/Underlining, Keyword Mnemonic, Imagery for Text, Rereading

### Cautions and Boundary Conditions

**For Practice Testing:**
- Feedback important - practice testing with feedback outperforms testing alone
- Recall-based formats (short answer, free recall) more effective than recognition-based (multiple choice)
- Potential error perseveration without feedback

**For Distributed Practice:**
- Optimal spacing depends on retention interval: best lag ≈ 10-20% of desired retention interval
- Example: To remember for 1 week, space 12-24 hours apart; for 5 years, space 6-12 months apart
- Longer lags not always better - depends on desired retention duration
- Effects smaller for very complex tasks

**For Interleaving:**
- May require initial blocked practice for basic competency
- Works best when exemplars from different categories are similar (discriminative contrast)
- Implementation challenges: takes more time than blocked practice; may slow during practice

## 2. Retrieval Practice / Testing Effect

### Roediger & Karpicke (2006): Foundational Study

**Exact Citation:**
Roediger, H. L., III, & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention. *Psychological Science*, *17*(3), 249-255. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x

**Key Finding:** Taking memory tests improves long-term retention compared to restudying

**Impact:** 4,900+ citations (highly influential)

### Recent Meta-Analyses (2015+)

**Rowland (2014) Meta-Analysis**
- "The Effect of Testing Versus Restudy on Retention: A Meta-Analytic Review of the Testing Effect"
- *Psychological Bulletin* - 1,600+ citations
- High-exposure: g = 0.66 (CI [0.56, 0.75])
- Single words: g = 0.33 (CI [0.16, 0.50])
- Other stimuli: g = 0.27 (CI [0.06, 0.48])
- Overall mean advantage: g = 0.50 (large effect)

**Adesope et al. (2017) Meta-Analysis**
- "Rethinking the Use of Tests: A Meta-Analysis of Practice Testing"
- *Review of Educational Research* - 928+ citations
- 118 studies examining retrieval practice
- Elementary students: g = 0.64 (moderate to large effect)

**Agarwal et al. (2021) Systematic Review**
- "Retrieval Practice Consistently Benefits Student Learning: A Systematic Review of Applied Research in Schools and Classrooms"
- *Educational Psychology Review* - 376+ citations
- 49 effect sizes, total N = 5,374
- 57% of effect sizes revealed medium or large benefits
- Overall effect: g = 0.60 (moderate-to-large)

**Schwieren et al. (2017)**
- Psychology classrooms: d = 0.56 for testing effect

### Recommendations for Practice Design

**Spacing:**
- Any spacing is better than none - whether days, weeks, or months
- Rule of thumb: "Put enough time in between something so it's not fresh in your mind"
- Space out retrieval practice into smaller sessions over time

**Frequency:**
- Use short bursts over time rather than single long sessions
- Distribute retrieval practice across multiple sessions (days, weeks, or months)
- Incorporate retrieval practice in the middle of lessons (not just start/end)
- 3 hours all at once vs. 30 minutes each day for 6 days = same total time, but spacing leads to significantly better learning

**Feedback:**
- Provide timely and constructive feedback to maximize learning effectiveness
- Confident errors are easier to correct than unsure ones (hypercorrection effect)
- Ask learners to rate confidence before giving feedback
- Correction of confident wrong answers strengthens memory more than uncertain ones

**Implementation:**
- Combine retrieval practice with spacing and interleaving for maximum effectiveness
- Use low-stakes quizzing to reduce test anxiety
- Implement frequent, ungraded tests (short quizzes, brain dumps)
- Pretesting primes the brain for learning and activates prior knowledge
- Failed retrieval followed by feedback creates lasting learning

## 3. Spacing Effect / Distributed Practice

### Cepeda et al. (2008): Optimal Spacing Intervals

**Key Finding:** Optimal lag is approximately 10-20% of desired retention interval

**Examples:**
- To remember for 1 week: space 12-24 hours apart
- To remember for 5 years: space 6-12 months apart

**Critical Insight:** Longer lags not always better - depends on how long learner wants to retain information

### Effect Sizes

**Cepeda et al. (2006) Meta-Analysis:**
- 254 studies with >14,000 participants
- Spaced study: 47% recall vs Massed study: 37% recall

**Donovan & Radosevich (1999) Meta-Analysis:**
- Moderate effect sizes for recall of verbal stimuli

**Janiszewski et al. (2003) Meta-Analysis:**
- Moderate effect sizes

**Mawson et al. (2025):**
- d = 0.54 for distributed practice

### Recommendations for Practice Design

**Optimal Intervals:**
- Target 10-20% of desired retention interval
- Any spacing is better than none
- Adjust based on retention goals and task complexity

**Implementation:**
- Break up lessons/units into smaller sessions over multiple days
- Intentional processing produces larger effect sizes than incidental processing
- Effects may be smaller for very complex tasks

## 4. Cross-Verified Synthesis

### Consistent Effect Sizes Across Mechanisms

All four approaches show **consistent moderate effects (d ≈ 0.5-0.6)**:

- Practice Testing: d = 0.50-0.56
- Distributed Practice: d = 0.54
- Worked Examples: d = 0.52 (from Crissman, 2006)
- Interleaving: 43% boost in accuracy

### Consensus on Practice Design

**No Fundamental Conflicts** - The approaches are synergistic:
- Retrieval practice + spaced practice = **Spaced Retrieval** (most effective combination)
- All three approaches show complementary rather than competing effects

**Unified Design Principle:**
1. Distribute retrieval opportunities across multiple sessions
2. Use worked examples for novices, fade as expertise develops
3. Combine strategies rather than choosing one

### Critical Boundary Conditions

**Worked Examples Fail When:**
- **Expertise Reversal Effect**: Advanced learners find guidance redundant
- Processing examples increases cognitive load for experienced learners
- **Solution**: Gradually fade worked examples as expertise increases

**Testing Effect Fails When:**
- Item difficulty mismatches learner level
- Integration levels create interference
- Retrieval performance conditions suboptimal

**Spacing Effect Fails When:**
- Initial practice performance moderates effectiveness
- Overlearning characterizes spacing scenarios
- Contextual variability insufficient

### Verified High-Confidence Practice Design

1. **Combine spaced retrieval with worked examples** (for novices)
2. **Space retrieval over days/weeks** rather than massing
3. **Adapt to learner expertise** (fade worked examples as skills develop)
4. **Match item difficulty** to learner level
5. **Use any spacing** rather than none for retrieval practice

## 5. Implementation Recommendations

### Core Principles

**Start Simple:**
- Begin with basic retrieval practice (short quizzes, brain dumps)
- Add spacing by distributing practice across multiple sessions
- Interleave related topics to boost discrimination

**Add Complexity Gradually:**
- Combine retrieval + spacing for maximum effect
- Use worked examples for novices, fade as expertise develops
- Adapt difficulty based on learner proficiency

**Provide Feedback:**
- Always include feedback after retrieval practice
- Ask confidence ratings before feedback (hypercorrection effect)
- Correct confident errors for maximum impact

### Practical Considerations

**Time Efficiency:**
- Short bursts over time > single long sessions
- 30 minutes/day for 6 days > 3 hours all at once
- Any spacing is better than none

**Learner Adaptation:**
- Match difficulty to learner level
- Consider prior knowledge and expertise
- Fade worked examples as skills develop

**Error Management:**
- Use low-stakes quizzing to reduce anxiety
- Provide immediate feedback to prevent error perseveration
- Confident errors are learning opportunities

## 6. Worked Examples and Scaffolding

### Most Comprehensive Recent Meta-Analysis

**Barbieri, C. A., Miller-Cotto, D., Clerjuste, S. N., & Chawla, K. (2023). "A meta-analysis of the worked examples effect on mathematics performance." *Educational Psychology Review*, 35, 11.**

**Effect Size:** g = 0.48 (medium effect); 95% CI [0.36, 0.60]

**Sample:** 43 articles, 55 studies, 181 effect sizes

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH

### Foundational Studies

**Sweller & Cooper (1985). "The use of worked examples as a substitute for problem solving in learning algebra." *Cognition and Instruction*, 2(1), 59-89.**

Finding: Students took **half as long** and made **1/5 as many errors**

**Crissman (2006). "The design and utilization of effective worked examples: A meta-analysis." *Doctoral Dissertation, University of Nebraska-Lincoln*.**

**Effect Size:** d = 0.70 for science education; d = 0.52 overall

### Specific Effect Sizes (Verifiable)

| Context | Effect Size | Metric | Study |
|---------|-------------|---------|-------|
| Mathematics performance | **g = 0.48** | Hedges' g | Barbieri et al. (2023) |
| Science education | **d = 0.70** | Cohen's d | Crissman (2006) |
| General learning | **d = 0.52** | Cohen's d | Crissman (2006) |
| Self-explanation | **d ≈ 2.0** | Cohen's d | Atkinson et al. (2000) |
| Online scaffolding | **g = 0.53** | Hedges' g | Zuo (2023) |

### Evidence-Based Recommendations

**Use Worked Examples When:**
- Learners are **novices** with low prior knowledge
- Teaching **well-structured domains** (math, physics, programming)
- During **initial knowledge acquisition phase**
- Goal is to **reduce learning time** and errors
- Using **correct examples** (β = 0.26, p = 0.03 positive impact)

**Avoid/Reduce When:**
- Learners are **experts** (expertise reversal effect)
- During **later practice phases** for advanced learners
- Domain is **ill-structured** with ambiguous solutions

### Critical Boundary Conditions

**Expertise Reversal Effect** (VERY HIGH confidence):
- Instructional techniques beneficial for novices become detrimental for experts
- Mechanism: Redundant information creates extraneous cognitive load
- **Key Implication:** Must adapt examples based on learner expertise

**Surprising Finding:**
- **Self-explanation prompts** showed negative moderation (β = -0.24, p = 0.04)
- May create redundancy effects or vary by prompt quality
- **Use with caution**

### Educational Applications with Evidence

**Strongest Evidence Base:**
- **Mathematics education** (60% of studies: algebra and geometry)
- **Computer-based learning** (scaffolding g = 0.385)
- **STEM education** (physics, chemistry, programming)

**Skill Acquisition Phases:**
1. **Initial phase:** Extensive worked examples
2. **Intermediate phase:** Adaptive fading procedures
3. **Advanced phase:** Problem-solving practice predominates

### Quality of Evidence

- **Total effect sizes analyzed:** 500+ across multiple meta-analyses
- **Heterogeneity:** I² = 93.72% (substantial variation across studies)
- **Publication bias:** Detected but effect remained positive after adjustment
- **Ecological validity:** 91% of studies were school-based

## Additional Spacing Effect Research

### Cepeda et al. (2008) Complete Citation

**Cepeda, N. J., Vul, E., Rohrer, D., Wixted, J. T., & Pashler, H. (2008). Spacing effects in learning: A temporal ridgeline of optimal retention. *Psychological Science*, 19(11), 1095-1102. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x**

**Key Study Details:**
- Sample: Over 1,350 participants
- Design: Participants learned facts and received a review after gaps up to 3.5 months, with final testing up to 1 year later
- Citation Count: 1,000+ citations

### Optimal Spacing Intervals - Specific Recommendations

**For 1-week retention delay:** Optimal gap = 20-40% of test delay (1.4-2.8 days)

**For 1-year retention delay:** Optimal gap = 5-10% of test delay (18-36.5 days)

**General principle:** Optimal inter-study interval = 10-20% of retention interval for most conditions

**Performance Improvements:**
- Recall performance: 64% improvement with optimal spacing
- Recognition performance: 26% improvement under optimal spacing

### Recent Meta-Analyses (2021-2025)

**Latimier et al. (2021). "A meta-analytic review of the benefit of spacing out retrieval practice episodes on retention." *Educational Psychology Review*, 33(3), 959–987.**

- Studies: 29 studies investigating spaced retrieval practice
- Effect Size: d = 0.85 for spaced learning with verbal contents (large effect)
- Citation Count: 149+ citations

**Murray et al. (2025). "A meta-analytic review of the effectiveness of spacing and retrieval practice for mathematics learning." *Educational Psychology Review*.**

- Studies: 27 studies with 53 effect sizes
- Effect Size: g = 0.28 (small to medium effect)
- Finding: Robust small to medium effect of spaced vs. massed practice in mathematics
- Context: Mathematics shows smaller spacing effects than other domains

**Bego et al. (2024). "Single-paper meta-analyses of the effects of spaced retrieval practice." *International Journal of STEM Education*.**

- Finding: Significant spacing effect when all courses were included
- Context: Effect not significant when calculus was excluded
- Heterogeneity: 89% across nine courses

### Spacing Effect Consensus

1. **General Effect Size Range:** d = 0.28 to 0.85 (small to large effects)
2. **Domain Differences:** Verbal content shows larger effects (d = 0.85) than mathematics (g = 0.28)
3. **Robustness:** Effect is robust across different contexts but varies by subject matter
4. **Optimal Timing:** 10-20% of desired retention interval provides optimal spacing
5. **Recent Consensus:** Meta-analyses from 2021-2025 continue to confirm the spacing effect with consistent effect sizes
